Abstract
The FRI protocol (ICALP ’18) is one of the most influential and widely deployed building
blocks at the core of modern SNARK systems. While its concrete security is well understood,
existing security proofs are intricate and technically complex.
In this work, we present a novel security analysis of FRI, in particular its round-by-
round soundness. This analysis yields additional insights about FRI that previous analysis
did not. Equally important, our approach is significantly simpler than previous proofs. This
makes it more accessible to a broader audience, lowering the barrier to understanding this
fundamental protocol. Furthermore, the relative simplicity of our analysis may pave the
way for future formal verification efforts of modern SNARK constructions.

Introduction
The FRI protocol [BBHR18] is a foundational component of modern cryptographic argument
systems, providing an efficient method for proving proximity to a Reed–Solomon (RS) code.
It lies at the core of an entire industry of succinct hash-based arguments in production. To
highlight this importance, note that this industry will play a critical role in securing billions of
dollars on Ethereum in the future1. Given its central importance, it is crucial to have a precise
and thorough understanding of FRI’s security guarantees.
A Novel Analysis of FRI.
While several analyses of FRI exist [BKS18, BCI+20, Sta21,
BGK+23, ABN23, AFR23, EA23] and its concrete security is well understood, the existing
proofs are technically complex and can be challenging to follow. In this work, we present a
novel, simpler and more accessible proof of round-by-round soundness for FRI, which is the
notion needed to deduce security of the non-interactive protocol that is built from it [BCS16,
CCH+19, Hol19]. This new analysis offers three main benefits:
• Accessibility for new researchers: By lowering the technical entry barrier, we aim to make
this area more approachable, encouraging new contributions and fresh ideas that could
shape the future of the field.
• A foundation for formal verification: Our simplified analysis can serve as a template for
formal verification efforts, which are increasingly important in the context of production
SNARK systems.
• Additional insights into FRI: Our novel proof also shows additional structural properties
of FRI, which may be of independent interest. For instance, they may find application
when applying FRI in the context of data availability sampling [HASW24] or knowledge
soundness.
The third property shows that our proof is not just a simplification of existing proofs, but yields
fundamentally new insights. We discuss these in Section 5.4 and compare our novel analysis to
previous approaches in Section 5.5.
Application in Formal Verification.
One of the ambitious goals of Ethereum’s post-
quantum roadmap is to achieve formal verification of every component in the stack [Eth25,
DHT+26]. We believe that our work contributes to this vision by making the security analysis
of a key protocol (namely, FRI) more tractable. Following the publication of a preprint of this
document, Hirai [Hir26] released a machine-assisted Lean4 formalization of our proof.
Mutual correlated agreement.
We note that our proof relies on the mutual correlated
agreement (MCA) property [ACY23, ACFY25], whereas standard analyses of FRI can be carried
out under the correlated agreement (CA) property. When we initially published this article,
MCA was a plausible conjecture believed by most domain experts, but stronger than CA. In
subsequent work [BCH+25, BCGM25, Hab25], the community made significant progress in
understanding MCA, and it is now known to hold in the same regimes as correlated agreement.
Interestingly, proofs of MCA use the same tools and proving strategies as those of CA.

Preliminaries
Most of the notation and preliminaries in this section are lifted almost verbatim from STIR and
WHIR [ACFY24, ACFY25]. As a quick reference, we use the following notation:
• The notation x ←X means that x is sampled uniformly at random from a finite set X.
• The “hat” symbol over a function (e.g., bp) indicates that it is a polynomial.
• Given a polynomial bp, we write degX(bp) to denote the degree of bp in the variable X. We
omit the subscript when bp is a univariate polynomial.
• For two functions f, g: L →Σ, we denote ∆(f, g) the fractional Hamming distance be-
tween f and g, i.e., the fraction of points in which they disagree.
• For a set S ⊆Σn, ∆(f, S) := minh∈S ∆(f, h).
• For a function f : L →Σ and a set S ⊆L, we write f[S] to denote the restriction of f to
the set S. In particular, for two such functions f, g, we have
f[S] = g[S] ⇐⇒∀s ∈S : f(s) = g(s).
• For a ternary relation R = {(x, y, w)}, let L(R) := {(x, y) | ∃w, (x, y, w) ∈R} be the
language induced by R.
• For a set L ⊆F and m ∈N, we define Lm := {xm : x ∈L}.
• We assume that all sets in this work admit some canonical ordering of their elements.

IOPPs and Round-By-Round Soundness
Before we move our attention to FRI and its new analysis, we recall the notion of IOPPs and
round-by-round soundness.
3.1
Interactive oracle proof of proximity
In an interactive oracle proof for a relation R, we consider a prover and a verifier, where
the prover wants to convince the verifier of the validity of some statement. To this end, the
prover can provide oracles to the verifier and the verifier can respond with challenges. The
verifier can additionally query the oracles provided by the prover. For convenience, we split
the statement into x, to which the verifier has full access, and into an oracle part y, to which
the verifier has oracle access. The prover also knows some witness w. Completeness states
that the honest prover convinces the verifier if (x, y, w) ∈R. Soundness instead bounds the
probability of the prover succeeding if (x, y) is a no-instance. For an interactive oracle proof of
proximity (IOPP), it is convenient to assume that x describes the parameters of a code, and y
describes an alleged codeword, and the soundness bound should depends only on the (Hamming)
distance of the provided y to the code specified by x. For this work, it is sufficient to consider
public-coin IOPPs, where the verifier’s messages are all uniformly sampled from prescribed sets
(that are statistically independent from the execution of the protocol). In this case, we denote
Vy,π1,...,πk(x, α1, . . . , αk+1) to be the deterministic function that has oracle access to y and the
prover messages, takes as input the verifier messages and outputs the verifier’s decision bit. We
now give the precise definition.

3.2
Round-by-round soundness
We recall the notion of round-by-round soundness [CCH+19]. Before we can define it, we first
define what a state function is. Intuitively, it associates every (partial) transcript with a state
that is either 0 or 1. Intuitively, a 0-state indicates that the prover is about to lose, i.e., the
verifier is about to reject. A malicious prover should start with state 0, and if in the end of the
interaction the state is still 0, then the verifier rejects.
Definition 3.4 (State function). Let (P, V) be an IOPP for a relation R = {(x, y, w)}. A
state function for (P, V) is a (possibly inefficient) function State that receives as inputs x, y,
and a transcript tr and outputs a bit, and has the following properties:
• Empty transcript: if tr = ∅is the empty transcript, then State(x, y, tr) = 1 if and only if
(x, y) ∈L(R).
• Prover moves: if tr is a transcript where the prover is about to move, and State(x, y, tr) =
0, then for every prover message π, State(x, y, tr||π) = 0.
• Full transcript: if tr is a full transcript and State(x, y, tr) = 0, then V rejects given this
interaction transcript.
• Default: whenever tr is any transcript and State(x, y, tr) = 1, then every extension tr′ of
tr satisfies State(x, y, tr′) = 1.
With this definition at hand, we now think about a malicious prover that wants to convince
the verifier. It starts in state 0 but must manage to end up in state 1, as otherwise the verifier
will reject. Thus, in at least one of the rounds, this malicious prover must be lucky in the
sense that the state changes from 0 to 1. Round-by-round soundness asserts that changing
the state from 0 to 1 happens only with small probability. A proof of proximity relaxes this
requirement and allows the probability of changing state to be large when the instance is close
to the language, as long as this probability decreases and becomes small when the instance is
far from the language.
Definition 3.5 (Round-by-round soundness). A k-round IOPP (P, V) for a relation R =
{(x, y, w)} has round-by-round soundness with error functions (ε1, . . . , εk) if the IOPP has a
state function State, such that for every x, y and transcript tr = (α1, π2, . . . , αi−1, πi),
Pr
αi [State(x, y, tr) = 0 ∧State(x, y, tr||αi) = 1] ≤εi(x, y).
As with standard soundness, we write εi as a function of distance when appropriate.
Clearly, by the above reasoning, round-by-round soundness implies standard soundness by
taking a union bound over all errors in the individual rounds. In practice, however, we are
interested in the non-interactive BCS-compiled [BCS16] protocol. The soundness of this non-
interactive protocol relates directly to round-by-round soundness and not to soundness. To get
an intuition for that, note that a malicious prover in the non-interactive setting can try to query
the random oracle that outputs the verifier messages multiple times per round. For instance,
it may try a few queries to find a good α1, then proceed to the next round and so on. With
soundness, we cannot directly bound the success probability of such a non-interactive prover,
as the interactive prover in the soundness experiment only gets one challenge per round. On
the other hand, round-by-round soundness can be used, and we briefly and informally sketch
the intuition here:

4
The FRI IOPP
In this section, we recall the FRI IOPP [BBHR18], before we give our new analysis in the next
section. We consider the setting in which a prover holds an initial function f0 : L0 →F, to
which the verifier has oracle access. Informally, FRI allows the verifier to test that this initial
function is close to some initial Reed-Solomon code C0, of dimension d0 and rate ρ = d0/|L0|.
We assume some basic familiarity with the FRI protocol and its associated tools. For a good
introduction, we refer to the lectures of Dan Boneh available online [Whi25a, Whi25b].
4.1
Folding univariate functions
We first recall some notation for folding univariate functions following notation of STIR and
WHIR [ACFY24, ACFY25]. Folding is one of the key tools in the FRI protocol as it allows to
reduce the size of a proximity testing problem by a factor m. Definition 4.2 below is given for
an arbitrary folding factor m. Readers may be more familiar with the case m = 2 where bpf,z is
computed from the odd and even coefficients of bf (in the honest case).
Definition 4.1 (Block). Let m ∈N be an integer and L ⊆F be a multiplicative coset of F∗
such that |L| is divisible by m. For each z ∈Lm, we define the set
Block(L, m, z) := {y ∈L : ym = z} ⊆L.
When L and m are clear from context, we write Block(z).
Definition 4.2 (Fold function). Let m ∈N be an integer and L ⊆F be a multiplicative coset
of F∗such that |L| is divisible by m. Let f : L →F be a function and α ∈F. For each z ∈Lm,
define bpf,z to be the unique polynomial of degree less than m with bpf,z(y) = f(y) for each
y ∈Block(z). Then, we define the function Fold(f, m, α): Lm →F as
∀z ∈Lm, Fold(f, m, α)(z) := bpf,z(α).
By definition, Fold(f, m, α)(z) can be computed by interpolation from the evaluations of f at
all points in Block(z).
Remark 4.3. It is well-known (cf. [BS05, ACFY24, ACFY25]) that if the function f is in
RS[F, L, d], then Fold(f, m, α) is in RS[F, Lm, d/m], for every α ∈F. This is important for the
completeness of FRI.

5
Our Proof
In this section, we present our new security analysis.
We start with an informal overview
in Section 5.1. We then state and prove a key lemma about folding univariate functions in
Section 5.2. Using this lemma, we give our formal proof in Section 5.3 and prove some additional
properties in Section 5.4.
Finally, we give a high-level comparison of our proof with exists
strategies in Section 5.5. We extend our analysis to the case of batched FRI in Section A.
5.1
Intuition and overview
To prove that FRI has round-by-round soundness, we first define a state function. Then, for
each round of interaction, we will bound the probability that a random verifier message changes
the state from 0 to 1. Before describing the state function, we introduce the graph of prover
messages, a useful tool in our reasoning which appears implicitly [BCI+20] or explicitly [BKS18,
ABN23] in previous analyses of FRI.
The prover message graph.
Consider an execution of the FRI protocol for the initial
function f0 with k rounds of folding. A full transcript for this interaction is of the form
tr = (α1, f1, . . . , αk, fk, αk+1),
where the values α1, . . . , αk and f1, . . . , fk are respectively the verifier and prover messages
during the folding rounds, and αk+1 defines the verifier’s queries to the oracles. By definition
of the FRI protocol, the functions f0, . . . , fk are defined over related domains (L0, . . . , Lk,
respectively) and are expected to be consecutive applications of the Fold function to f0.
We can draw a layered graph in which the i-th layer represents the i-th evaluation domain
Li, and each node of the layer corresponds to a point y ∈Li. We draw an edge between nodes
x ∈Li−1 and y ∈Li if and only if xmi = y, or equivalently x ∈Block(y). In this case, we follow
typical graph conventions and call y the parent node and x the child node; children nodes with
the same parent node are called siblings. An illustration of this graph for |L0| = 16, k = 3 and
mi = 2 for all i is given in3 Figure 1.
Coloring the graph.
We will now color the graph to reflect the verifier’s observations and
its eventual output. During the query phase of FRI, the verifier reads the final function fk in
full and runs tests to assert that the rest of the graph is consistent with it. The verifier samples
a node s(0) ∈L0 and defines a path of related queries s(1) ∈L1, . . . , s(k) ∈Lk that traverses
the graph vertically. Roughly, our coloring therefore considers all such paths and marks them
as red if any of the verifier’s assertions fail, and green otherwise. More concretely, the coloring
rules are defined below and illustrated in Figure 2:
1. Base case. The bottom layer is always green.
2. Inductive rule. Given a node x ∈Li−1 at layer i −1 with parent y ∈Li:
(a) if the parent y is red, then x is red. This reflects the fact that any verification path
that goes through x will also go through xmi = y.
(b) if the parent y is green, compute Fold(fi−1, mi, αi)(y) from x and its siblings and
i. color x green if an only if Fold(fi−1, mi, αi)(y) = fi(y) (i.e., if the verifier’s
assertion passes).
ii. color x red otherwise (i.e., if the verifier’s assertion fails).

State function.
By definition, we need to set the initial state State(f0, ∅) to be 0 if and only
if f0 /∈C0. We now separate the description of the state function between the folding rounds
and the query round.

• Folding rounds. As noted in our intuition for the prover message graph, the verifier will reject
all queries that induce a path through red nodes. Therefore, during the folding phase, we can
focus on the subgraph that contains paths that exclusively traverse green nodes; trusting that
all other paths will be rejected in the query phase. This allows us to reason only in terms of
honestly folded functions.
For convenience, we refer to this accepting subgraph as the green subgraph and denote Wi
the set of nodes at the i-th layer of the green subgraph. In Figure 2, this corresponds to all
nodes that are green and not marked with an X.
Ideally, we would like to establish that if Fold(fi−1, mi, αi) agrees with a codeword ui ∈Ci on
the green subset Wi ⊆Li, then fi−1 agrees with a codeword ui−1 ∈Li−1 on Wi−1 ⊆Li−1. In
other words, that:
(ideal property) “agreement propagates upwards through the green subgraph.”
Unfortunately, this property does not always hold true. We can, however, show that a relaxed
version of this implication holds with high probability using mutual correlated agreement. Our
relaxed ideal property becomes
(relaxed ideal property) “if all layers of the green subgraph are large enough, then
agreement propagates upwards through the green subgraph.”
The notion of large enough is a parameter of our analysis. We let θ ∈[0, 1] be an arbitrary
parameter and say that a layer Wi ⊆Li is large enough if |Wi| ≥(1 −θ) · |Li|. As we will see,
θ imposes a tradeoff between the analysis of the folding rounds and that of the query round.
We can accept a relaxation of the ideal property because if any layer of the green subgraph is
small (i.e., not large enough), then the red subset at that layer must be large. These red nodes
will propagate upwards and cause the verifier to reject with high probability. We discuss this
further in the analysis section for the query phase.
We define the state function such that it indicates whether the relaxed ideal property holds
for a given initial function f0 and transcript tr: when all the honest folded functions are safe
(as per our relaxed ideal property), we set State(f0, tr) = 0; when there is an honest fold
that is exploitable (one for which all green subgraph layers are large but agreement does not
propagate upward), we set State(f0, tr) = 1. The event “the state changed from 0 to 1 at
round i” is captured by the following semi-formal description. Given an initial function f0
and a transcript tr := (α1, f1, . . . , αi−1, fi−1) such that State(f0, tr) = 0, and an i-th verifier
message αi, we say that State(f0, tr||αi) = 1 if and only if there exists a set S ⊆Li−1 such
that:
1. Smi is large enough. Specifically, |Smi| ≥(1 −θ) · |Li|. Note that this implies that S
itself is large enough.
2. The honest fold of fi−1 agrees with a codeword u ∈Ci on the set Smi. In other words,
there is agreement with the code on this large enough set.
3. There are no codewords in Ci−1 that agree with fi−1 on the set S. Effectively, that
agreement does not propagate upwards through the large enough set of green nodes.
• Query round. The query round is the final round of the protocol. Therefore, by the “full
transcript” property of the state function (see Definition 3.4), the output of the state function
at this round must be equivalent to the verifier’s final decision. We therefore set the state
function to be 1 if and only if all the verifier checks pass.

5.4
Additional properties
Usually, the property that FRI is supposed to provide is the following: if a verifier accepts a
FRI interaction, then the initial function was somewhat close to a Reed–Solomon codeword
(except with negligible probability). Our round-by-round soundness analysis allows to surface
this property.
As we will show here, our analysis also shows additional properties.
These
properties may be used in the future as a stepping stone, e.g., towards proving round-by-round
knowledge soundness [CMS19, BCFW25] or towards constructing efficient code commitments
and data availability sampling schemes [HASW24].
More concretely, we show in Corollary 5.6 that, under certain conditions, a FRI transcript
guarantees that the prover has committed to a Reed-Solomon codeword. This codeword agrees
with the initial function on a large subset of its domain; hence proving proximity. Furthermore,
the commitment is binding in the sense that any attempt to open it at a point where the initial
function disagrees with the committed codeword results in rejection by the verifier. We note
that morally, this property is similar to opening-consistency in [HASW24], but in contrast to
their work, our analysis is not restricted to the unique decoding regime4. Consequently, we
believe that this can be a starting point for future work towards data availability sampling from
FRI beyond the unique decoding radius.

5.5
Comparison to existing proof strategies
Let us put our proof into context by comparing it with other analyses.
Proofs using CA.
The soundness of FRI is first shown in the eponymous paper [BBHR18].
The error bounds were later improved by Ben-Sasson, Kopparty and Saraf [BKS18] and Ben-
Sasson et al. [BCI+20]. Our proof follows the ideas of Ben-Sasson et al. [BCI+20] and Augot,
Bordage and Nardi [ABN23] but uses mutual correlated agreement (MCA) to analyze folding
rounds. In contrast, our two references use a weaker notion known as correlated agreement
(CA), or plain correlated agreement to avoid confusion. Their proofs achieve the same bounds
as ours, however we consider their reasoning to be conceptually harder to grasp. The main
difference lies in the guarantee that folding provides in the case when are challenges are safe
(i.e., state remains 0):
• MCA: if all green subsets are large enough, then agreement propagates upwards through
green nodes.
• CA: if all green subsets are large enough, then agreement propagates upwards through
layers at unknown nodes.
The latter loses information about where the agreement is located. Furthermore, agreement
may not be aligned with green nodes.
Overcoming these hurdles requires the introduction
of additional tools, notably a notion of weighted distance (rather than standard Hamming
distance) and a correlated agreement theorem for this weighted distance.
The ethSTARK
documentation [Sta21] and the work by Block et al. [BGK+23] show that the analysis given by
Ben-Sasson et al. [BCI+20] is in fact a proof of the round-by-round soundness of FRI.
Do we gain anything?
One may wonder whether using MCA rather than CA allows us
to prove that FRI has more bits of security per query. Unfortunately not; as stated above,
our upper bounds on the round errors is identical to previous analyses. On the other hand,
we prove that FRI satisfies stronger properties in Corollary 5.6. Our analysis is the first to
show that FRI’s folding phase (also known as the “commit” phase) is indeed a binding com-
mitment to some Reed-Solomon codeword. A restricted version of this fact is implicitly shown
in FRIDA [HASW24] to construct a code commitment from FRI.
Other proofs.
The security of FRI is also discussed in works that introduce new frameworks
as a means of illustrating the power of those frameworks. Attema, Fehr and Resch [AFR23]
prove that an interactive proof derived from FRI (using Merkle commitments to replace the
prover’s oracles) meets their notion of generalized special soundness. Evans and Angeris [EA23]
prove the security of a variant of FRI using only the tools of linear algebra. In both cases, the
analyses provide looser bounds and are constrained to a smaller set of FRI parameters.

an IOPP for the interleaved Reed-Solomon code C0≡b as defined below.
Interleaved codes.

RS[F, L, d]≡b := {(g1, . . . , gb) : ∀i ∈[b], gi ∈RS[F, L, d]} .
